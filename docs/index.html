Where Northwind is growing, who it depends on, what discounts cost, and what may run out

Question 1

Which customer markets are growing or declining, and where should sales attention go?

Short answer. Yes, in most places. Revenue in Jan–Apr 1998 was 2.2× the same months of 1997, and 17 of 21 markets grew. The USA and Germany grew across many customers; several other markets rest on a single customer.

Why it matters

Sales time is limited and revenue has more than doubled. The manager needs to know which markets deserve more attention, and which gains are fragile.

Approach

Add up net revenue by the customer's country for Jan–Apr 1998 and for Jan–Apr 1997. Only these months can be compared fairly: the data start in July 1996 and end on 6 May 1998. Then, for each country, check how much of the gain came from its single largest customer.

Main finding

  • Revenue more than doubled, because of more orders. Net revenue was $422.3k against $191.3k (2.2×). Orders rose from 123 to 256 (+108%); the average order only moved from $1,555 to $1,650 (+6%). Active customers rose from 65 to 80.
  • Growth is widespread. 17 of 21 markets grew. USA, Germany, Brazil, Austria and Sweden supplied 74% of the growth.
  • Some growth is solid. USA, Germany and UK grew across several accounts; in each, the largest account supplied under half of the gain.
  • Some growth is fragile. 6 of the 11 markets that grew by $5,000 or more got at least 70% of that growth from one account: Brazil, Austria, Sweden, Ireland, Venezuela and Spain.
  • 4 markets shrank (Finland, Mexico, Canada and Denmark), by $18,062 in total.

Business implication

Give sales capacity first to the markets where growth is large and spread across customers: USA and Germany together delivered 44% of all growth. For the single-account markets, the priority is protection: assign an owner to each lead account and recruit a second buyer, because losing one customer would erase most of that market's gain. Review the shrinking markets before they turn into lost ones; Denmark's drop is the largest.

Limits

Percent changes on small bases (for example Sweden and Spain) are unreliable, so the chart ranks dollar change. Four months of overlap cannot show seasonality, and a market with one or two customers can move on a single order.

How we got this

Show the calculation, step by step
  1. Revenue of one order line = unit price × quantity × (1 − discount). All 2,155 lines add up to $1,265,793.
  2. Keep only orders dated Jan–Apr of each year and group them by the customer's country. Jan–Apr 1997 adds up to $191,322 and Jan–Apr 1998 to $422,290. $422,290 ÷ $191,322 = 2.21×, a gain of $230,968.
  3. Example, USA: $84,708 − $31,518 = $53,190. That is $53,190 ÷ $230,968 = 23% of all growth.
  4. Largest account in the USA: Save-a-lot Markets supplied 38% of the USA's gain, about $20,330. The share is only shown for markets that grew by at least $5,000, because a share of a tiny change means little.
  5. A market counts as resting on one account when it grew by $5,000 or more and its largest account supplied at least 70% of that gain.
  6. Percent change is left blank when the 1997 revenue was under $2,500, because a tiny starting point makes the percentage swing wildly. The chart ranks dollar change instead.

Question 2

How dependent is revenue on a few customers, and which important accounts have gone quiet?

Short answer. Moderately, and not getting worse. The top 3 customers are 25% of revenue and that share is not rising. One top-10 customer, Mère Paillarde, has gone quiet and is worth a call.

Why it matters

Losing a top account hits revenue hard, and a regular buyer who stops ordering is easiest to win back early. Both are risks the manager can act on.

Approach

Rank customers by their total net revenue and see how much the top few contribute. Check whether that share is rising by comparing the same months of the two years. Then flag customers whose time since their last order is much longer than their own usual time between orders.

Main finding

  • Concentration is moderate. The three largest customers, QUICK-Stop, Ernst Handel and Save-a-lot Markets, produce 25% of lifetime revenue (the largest alone, 8.7%). The top 10 produce 45%, and 34 of 89 customers produce 80%.
  • Dependence is not rising. In Jan–Apr, the top 10 accounted for 54.2% of revenue in 1997 and 54.1% in 1998, even though active customers grew from 65 to 80.
  • One quiet account matters. Mère Paillarde (Canada) is the number 9 customer by lifetime revenue ($28,872). It normally orders about every 18 days and has been silent for 188 days.
  • The other flagged accounts are small. All 8 flagged accounts together are 3.4% of last-12-month revenue ($29,068). Seven Seas Imports (UK, 91 days) and Victuailles en stock (France, 103 days) are only just past their threshold. The 3 accounts with one or two orders are more likely one-off buyers than lapses.

Business implication

Concentration is moderate and stable, so it does not need an emergency response. Give each of the top three accounts a named owner and check their service levels regularly. Have someone call Mère Paillarde this week: it represents 1.5% of last-12-month revenue ($12,912), and winning back a regular buyer is cheaper than finding a new one. Re-run the lapse check monthly; customers.lapsed_accounts produces the list.

Limits

“Today” is the date of the last order (6 May 1998). The lapse rule is a judgement call; other thresholds would change the list. Customers with fewer than three orders have no reliable usual gap.

How we got this

Show the calculation, step by step
  1. Add each customer's net revenue over the whole period and rank them. Number 1 is QUICK-Stop with $110,277: $110,277 ÷ $1,265,793 = 8.7% of all revenue.
  2. Keep a running total down the ranking. The top 3 reach 25.2%, the top 10 reach 45.0%, and it takes 34 customers to reach 80%.
  3. Trend check: repeat the top-10 share using only Jan–Apr orders of each year: 54.2% in 1997 and 54.1% in 1998.
  4. Lapse rule: list each customer's order dates and take the gaps between them. The usual gap is the median gap, used only for customers with 3 or more orders. The threshold is the larger of 90 days and 3 × the usual gap. A customer is flagged when the days since their last order, counted up to 6 May 1998, exceed the threshold. With fewer than 3 orders there is no usual gap, so only the 90-day floor applies.
  5. Example, Mère Paillarde: usual gap 17.5 days, so 3 × 17.5 = 52.5. The threshold is the larger of 90 and 52.5, which is 90. It has been silent 188 days, more than 90, so it is flagged.
  6. Closest call, Seven Seas Imports: usual gap 29.0 days, threshold 90, silent 91 days. It is over the line by 1 day(s), so it is a weak flag.
  7. Last-12-month revenue is net revenue from orders in the 365 days up to the last order date.

Question 3

Do bigger discounts sell more, or do they just give revenue away?

Short answer. Discounted lines are bigger, but that is mostly because bigger orders get the discounts, and deeper discounts do not sell more. About $58,483 was given away beyond a 5% discount.

Why it matters

Discounts are the lever sales staff control most directly. They cost $88,653 over the period, 6.5% of gross sales, and 39% of order lines carry one.

Approach

Compare the units on order lines at each discount level (0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25%). Because buyers who order more may also get more discounts, the discount has to be separated from the buyer. So the analysis also compares discounted and full-price lines inside the same order, and checks product by product. Finally it adds up the discount dollars given at each level.

Main finding

  • Discounted lines are bigger. They average 27.4 units against 21.7 at full price (+26%; likely range +14% to +39%).
  • But the discount may not be the reason. Full-price lines in orders that also contain a discounted line already average 24.2 units, against 21.3 in orders with no discount. Bigger orders are the ones that get discounts.
  • Inside the same order the gap is small. In the 163 orders with both kinds of line, the discounted line is larger by 1.8 units (+8%). The likely range, -1.9 to +5.8 units, includes zero, so “no effect” cannot be ruled out. Product mix does not explain the gap either: 74% of the 77 products sell more per line when discounted, so buyer and order size matter more.
  • Deeper discounts do not sell more. A 5% discount averages 28.0 units and a 25% discount 28.2. The difference, +0.2 units, has a likely range of -4.9 to +4.7 (-18% to +17%). A statistical test finds no difference between the five levels (p = 0.29).
  • A 25% discount needs 27% more units per line than a 5% discount just to bring in the same revenue. Even the top of the likely range falls short of this.
  • Cost climbs with depth. The 25% level gave away $32,980 (37% of all discount dollars) against $7,769 (9%) at 5%. In total, $58,483 (66% of discount dollars, 4.6% of net revenue) went to the part of discounts above 5%.

Business implication

Set 5% as the standard discount and require approval above it: the extra depth shows no volume return, so most of the $58,483 should stay in revenue. Treat that figure as an upper bound. The data also do not show that a 5% discount itself raises order size once the same order is compared. Run a one-quarter test: randomly hold a share of discounted customers or reps at 5%, and another share at no discount, then compare order sizes before making any policy permanent.

Limits

This is observational. Discounts are not assigned at random, and larger orders are the ones that get them, which is why the same-order comparison matters. That comparison uses only the 163 orders that mix both kinds of line, so its range is wide and cannot rule out an effect of a few units. The ranges come from resampling customers with a fixed seed. There are no cost figures, so the savings are revenue, not profit. 8 lines with other discounts (1-4% and 6%) on one to three units are left out of the level analysis.

How we got this

Show the calculation, step by step
  1. Round each discount to two decimals (the sheet stores 5% as 0.0500000007) and keep the six standard levels: 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25%. 8 lines with other discounts are set aside, leaving 2,147 lines.
  2. Average the units per order line at each level (the table above). The range in the table is a standard 95% interval that treats each line as independent. The headline ranges in the findings are stricter: they resample whole customers 2,000 times with a fixed seed, because one customer's lines resemble each other.
  3. Discounted against full price: 27.4 ÷ 21.7 − 1 = +26%.
  4. Same-order comparison: in each of the 163 orders that have both kinds of line, take the average units of the discounted lines minus the average units of the full-price lines, then average those 163 differences: +1.8 units. The discounted line was larger in 86 orders and smaller in 72; the rest were equal. A sign test gives p = 0.30.
  5. Depth: 25% lines against 5% lines is 28.2 − 28.0 = +0.2 units. Across all five discount levels, the Kruskal-Wallis test gives p = 0.29. A p-value above 0.05 means the differences could easily be chance.
  6. Break-even: at 5% off, a unit brings in 0.95 of its price; at 25% off, 0.75. To bring in the same revenue per line, units must rise by 0.95 ÷ 0.75 − 1 = 26.7%.
  7. Discount dollars = unit price × quantity × discount, added up for each level (last two table columns). The total is $88,653, 6.5% of gross sales. Dollars “above 5%” count only the part of each discount over 5 points: unit price × quantity × (discount − 5%), which adds up to $58,483.

Question 4

Which fast-selling products are about to run out, and are we discontinuing products customers still buy?

Short answer. 28 of 67 active products would run out within 2 weeks at their current sales rate, and 4 discontinued products that customers still buy are already out of stock.

Why it matters

A stock-out loses the sale and can lose the customer. With orders doubling, stock levels set for slower months may no longer fit.

Approach

Estimate how many units of each product sell per week, using the last 120 days of orders. Divide the units in stock by that weekly rate to get “weeks of cover”, with and without units already on order. Flag active products under 2 weeks, and list discontinued products that still sell.

Main finding

  • 28 of 67 active products with recent sales (42%) have under 2 weeks of stock. They account for 62% of the weekly revenue of active products ($12,651 a week).
  • 16 of them have nothing on order, worth $9,660 a week. Côte de Blaye is the largest: $3,028 a week with 1.5 weeks of stock and no order placed.
  • 8 discontinued products still sell, 15% of weekly revenue ($3,620). 4 of them have no stock left (Thüringer Rostbratwurst, Alice Mutton, Perth Pasties and Chef Anton's Gumbo Mix), which is $2,897 a week of demand with nothing to sell.

Business implication

Place orders first for the best-selling products with nothing on order: Côte de Blaye, Camembert Pierrot, Tarte au sucre, Uncle Bob's Organic Dried Pears and Manjimup Dried Apples. Then compare reorder levels with current sales rates, since order volume has roughly doubled year over year. Decide deliberately about the discontinued products customers still buy: either reinstate the ones with steady sales (for example Thüringer Rostbratwurst, $1,965 a week) or tell customers what replaces them.

Limits

The Product sheet has no date, so stock levels are assumed current as of the last order. If stock has been replenished since, the risk is overstated. Weeks of cover use the last 120 days of sales, a period of fast growth; cover based on the full history would look healthier. Units on order have no arrival dates, so the light bars show the best case.

How we got this

Show the calculation, step by step
  1. Take the orders from the last 120 days of the data (7 Jan 1998 to 6 May 1998).
  2. Weekly units for a product = units sold in those 120 days ÷ 17.1 weeks. Weekly revenue is worked out the same way from net revenue.
  3. Weeks of cover = units in stock ÷ weekly units. Example, Côte de Blaye: 17 in stock ÷ 11.7 a week = 1.5 weeks, with 0 units on order.
  4. A product is flagged when it is active, has sold in the window and has under 2 weeks of cover. “Nothing on order” means the Product sheet shows 0 units on order. The 28 flagged products add up to $12,651 a week, 62% of the weekly revenue of active products.
  5. A product counts as discontinued and still selling when its Discontinued flag is on and it sold at least one unit in the window.

Plain-English guide

Net revenue
What customers paid: unit price × quantity × (1 − discount). Freight is not included.
Same months of both years
The data start in July 1996 and end in early May 1998, so full years cannot be compared. Growth compares January to April of 1998 with January to April of 1997.
Likely range (95% interval)
A range that should contain the true value about 95 times in 100. If the range includes zero, the data cannot rule out “no effect”.
p-value
How easily the result could come from chance alone. Above 0.05 means the difference could easily be chance.
Weeks of cover
Units in stock divided by units sold per week: how long the stock lasts at the current sales rate.
Silent or lapsed account
A customer whose time since their last order is far longer than their own usual time between orders.

Check our work

Every number on this page can be traced back to the data.

  1. See the data. The numbers come from the Northwind workbook: Google Sheet, or the Excel copy in the repository (data/Northwind_Database.xlsx).
  2. Check the total in a spreadsheet. In the Orders Details tab, type this into any empty cell: =SUMPRODUCT('Orders Details'!C2:C2156, 'Orders Details'!D2:D2156, 1-ROUND('Orders Details'!E2:E2156, 2)). It should show $1,265,793. Counting the rows in the Orders tab should give 830 orders.
  3. Open every number. Each “How we got this” block above lists the exact table and code file, and results.json holds every figure quoted on this page.
  4. Read the code. run_all.py runs the analysis; each question has its own module. README.md explains how to rebuild everything, and DOCSTRING_REPORT.md documents every function.
  5. See the independent checks. verify_with_sql.py recomputes the headline numbers with SQL in a separate database, and tests/test_analysis.py tests the logic.

Data and method notes

  • Revenue is unit price × quantity × (1 − discount) from the order lines. Freight is excluded. The price charged on the order line is used because Product.Price differs on 31% of lines.
  • Coverage. 830 orders and 2,155 order lines from 4 Jul 1996 to 6 May 1998; 89 of 91 customers have ordered. 1996 starts in July and 1998 ends in early May, so growth uses matching months.
  • Country is the customer's country. 99.5% of orders ship to the same country.
  • No cost data. Every figure is revenue, not profit.
  • Also checked: delivery. 95.4% of 809 shipped orders met their required date (37 late). Late rates run from 3.6% to 5.1% across the three shippers, a difference that could easily be chance (chi-square p = 0.68). Freight is 5.1% of net revenue, and 21 orders were still open. Fulfillment is not where to spend attention. Table: side_check_shippers.csv.
  • Verification. verify_with_sql.py recomputes the headline numbers with SQL in a separate in-memory database and compares them with the pandas results.

To rebuild every number, figure and table, run python run_all.py from the repository root.
